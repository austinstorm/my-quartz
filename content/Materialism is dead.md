> The second factor that encouraged a general understanding of the problem of interaction is the development of classical mechanics by Isaac Newton, who had a profound influence on Hume’s scepticism and Kant’s eschewal of the world as it is in itself. Newton effectively showed that materialism was moribund, if not already dead. He explicitly rejected indivisible atoms and his hypothesis of universal gravitation, a long-range instantaneous relation, came with no contact mechanism. For Newton, science should content itself with the mathematical framing of laws unburdened by assumptions of materialism; indeed, from a materialist perspective, gravity appeared occult.

> Yet, many people still call themselves materialists. This label is anachronistic at best, for materialism is dead, if understood as a 17th Century doctrine. What people seem to intend is a doctrine often referred to as physicalism, which claims that what is real is either what physics establishes or what can be reduced to (/explained by) physics. Note the difference. While old-school materialism was essentially a metaphysical doctrine, to which science was expected to conform prior to Newton, physicalism insists on the converse, that our metaphysics must conform to science (well, physics). This supposed adherence to science can be questioned, for it hardly follows from any particular science that there is a unified physical realm. Let this quibble pass, though. As regards the mind, at any rate, the doctrine is that if minds are real, they are really something else, the kind of stuff physics could handle, at least in principle.
