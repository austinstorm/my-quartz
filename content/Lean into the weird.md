### How do you market a secret society?

The Masonic Lodge in Moscow, ID started a drive to raise awareness and recruiting last year. The slogan of the campaign: "Secret Society? Not so much..."

It's hard out there for a fraternal society, and I don't blame them for trying to boost their numbers. One might imagine that *secret* societies are at a particular disadvantage when it comes to marketing.

But I think this is wrong, and I think this campaign is going in the exact *opposite* direction. This is following a current cultural trend: social media is awash in content that depicts people going into closed ritual and communal spaces that we would not normally have access to.
### Get ready with me to go to a Coptic Orthodox church!

If I wanted to visit a Coptic Orthodox church, I would have to drive two hours to Pasco, WA. And I would have to wait for the handful of times a year that they actually hold services. It also wouldn't be a very good representative example of the fullness of Coptic Orthodox practice - their building is an unassuming International-style cinderblock box, and the service is likely to be modest.

But now, thanks to the Internet, I can see an array of "Visit a Coptic Orthodox church with me!" videos.

There are many things we could say about this Spectacle and the way it may exaggerate our sense of access to or familiarity with Other People's Traditions. But for the moment, it is sufficient to say that these videos reveal a keen interest in communal ritual life.
### Lean into the weird!

What I would suggest is that more 'closed' or structured communities should lean into the work required to learn about them and participate in them. Instead of de-emphasizing the fact that they are a 'secret society', the Masons should *lean into it*.

There are implications for my own Anglican church context. In our service booklet we say "The Prayer of the Day," where the prayer book would put it "The Collect of the Day". The collect is pronounced funny - CALL-ekt - but it means more or less what you might assume: it's the collected prayers of the people gathered into one prayer. I believe it comes from monks, who after reciting the Psalm together would have one monk summarize the Psalm into a devotional summary called a collect.

I can understand why one might change 'collect' to 'prayer' in the interest of accessibility. But 'accessibility' means reducing things to a shallower version of themselves, and I would argue that people do not want this! They want depth and crunchiness and edges. People cannot own something unless they have to work for it, unless they experience it from the inside.

Instead of trying to reduce the barrier to entry, we should ask how our culture can maintain and emphasize its distinctives while also inviting people to the work of inhabiting them.
