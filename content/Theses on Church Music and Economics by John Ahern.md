I am for sure unclear about No 1-3, in light of my (realatively recent) suspicion of [[BHAGs]]. But I could be convinced otherwise!

> 10. Under the current conditions of music’s possibility, to do music, “to musick,” is to listen to other people do it. Our music is always-already a vicarious experience and a professionalized experience. It cannot be otherwise without changing the conditions of music’s possibility in America, without changing the economics of music.


This goes with James Jordan's critique from his Theses on Worship:

> If the choir is supplementing the congregation, or engaging in
> antiphonal responses with the congregation, I have no problem. The same with soloists. Such a "liturgical"
> use adds to worship. In our evangelical churches, however, the choir does not stand to the side singing in
> dialogue with the congregation, but stands in front to entertain. The choir "number" feels like
> entertainment, and solo singing feels even more 1ike it.


To get back to a more participatory music culture seems wild.

> 14. it is the duty and privilege of all Christians to make music (Eph 5:18, Col. 3:16). It is both a priestly and prophetic activity to make music (1 Chr. 25:1-3, 1 Chr. 25:5; 2 Chr. 29:30). The New Testament calls upon Christians universally and particularly to embrace these functions (Rev 1:6);


> 26. throughout all ages, beauty in the church has been a source of Sabbath, of what Josef Pieper calls leisure, for those whose lives tend to be enslaved by their labor or alienated from their labor. Beauty is not an escape but a goal of human labor; beauty within the church brings the needy to the end of their labors.


> 30. Church music is the enemy of “aesthetics” as it is often understood. For, as soon as you think art (and church music particularly) is autonomous self-expression, or a subjective universality, or unmediated access to the noumenal, you implicitly make the end of all worldly life the toil of work, and beauty an escape for those privileged enough to enjoy it. The critics of Arnold and Scruton are entirely correct: high art *is* classist and elitist, but only because it is first Manichean, despising all things this-worldly as irredeemable.
