“If we are to be prepared to live for Christ in the whole of life, we need to be singing about the whole of life.  In this, the Psalms, the only divinely authored hymnal in history is both our guide and our challenge. The Psalms are songs to God, about God, sung in community with the people of God. Through the centuries they have been the greatest source of inspiration for the writing and singing of hymns. The Psalms are our best resource for teaching us what to sing about, and how to apply the Gospel to every season of life.

Tim and Kathy Keller share, ‘The songs of Jesus are not just a matchless primer of teaching, but a medicine chest for the heart and the best possible guide for practical living.’”

- Keith and Kristyn Getty, 21st-century hymn writers

Writing on Augustine: “Singing the Psalms becomes a means of learning what it is to inhabit the Body of Christ and to be caught up in Christ’s prayer.”

“The singing of the Psalms becomes the most immediate routine means of identifying with the voice of Christ.”

- Rowan Williams, Anglican

“Protestants have learned to sing everything else- from sacred country music to sacred rock- except the psalms! …Individually we may not be able to identify with all of a given psalm’s moods and feelings, but we can be sure that somewhere in the body of Christ there are members who can. Therefore we pray the psalms in the spirit of Romans 12:15: ‘Rejoice with those who rejoice; mourn with those who mourn.’ In singing the psalms, we become aware of being a part of the church catholic.”

- Simon Chan, Pentecostal theologian

“That they have for centuries held a central place in Christian liturgies is good enough reason for highly esteeming the psalms. But they are more than a tradition, a treasured antique. Their significance lies deeper. The church has always valued them as a vehicle of prayer.”

“In two ways at least the psalms have proved to be a means of corporate prayer. First, as a microcosm of the whole Bible, they both set Christ before our minds and draw us into the prayer of Christ and that of the whole church. Second, they provide a language for liturgical prayer…

The spontaneous language which articulates the devotion of an individual may, at any particular moment, be too intense for some and too restrained for others. In the psalms is available an objective language which lifts us above ourselves without leaving us in the air. Each individual may not be able to make every word of the day’s psalmody his or her own. We know not how to pray as we ought (Romans 8:28), but here are words of scripture, given by the Holy Spirit, which help us to pray together as the church.”

“Christian interpretation of the psalms is not based on the subjectivity of the reader but in the history of salvation. It is because the psalms are rooted in real experience, and express the feelings and frustrations of struggling, sinful humanity - creatures of flesh and blood - that they enable us to come to God just as we are, without pretence, in prayer for that saving grace which only the love of Christ can supply.”

- Reginald Box, Anglican

“Things happen when you use the whole cycle [of psalms] which I think are less likely to happen when you only use part or skip back and forth in obedience to your own principle of selection, when actually the compiler, I think, did a pretty darn good job. … This, I think, is part of what it means to live as a community or an individual under the authority of Scripture: Allow the hymnbook God has given us to be the means of personal and communal transformation and growth. It was good enough for Jesus. I suggest it’s good enough for us.”

- N.T. Wright, Anglican
