> I saw a post the other day where someone said “it is so sad when a Protestant goes to purgatory because they don’t have people praying for them”—presumably because the more people that pray, the more vicarious satisfaction is made for them. But this perfectly captures the Protestant concern. *Christ* is interceding for them. And for Roman Catholics, that literally is not enough to clear their debt of punishment all at once.
>
> One might say “I can make the same objection with respect to prayer. Christ is praying for us. So why do others need to pray for us?” But this misses a distinction in Roman Catholic theology itself between meriting for another and satisfying for another:
>
>> Also, merit differs from impetration and satisfaction. For merit moves another by means of service; but impetration by means of a humble profession of one’s own need and the request for mercy; satisfaction moves by means of compensation for an offense against another. Hence, just as impetration implies need, so satisfaction implies an offense. But merit prescinds from both of these. (Severino González Rivas, *Sacrae Theologiae Summa IIIB: On Grace · On the Infused Virtues*, trans. Kenneth Baker (Saddle River, NJ: Keep the Faith, 2014 p.222-223)
>
> The issue is this. If I offer a compensation on behalf of another, God might give *more* than that compensation is worth. He doesn’t give less. Yet Christ, when he intercedes, offers *himself* as satisfaction for us. The Roman Catholic would have us believe that Christ is the *one person in reality to whom God gives less than the dignity of what is offered*.
>
> “Meriting” for another works differently, as “merit” at its most basic root just describes a general fittingness. Hence it is more fitting for God to answer prayer when Christ prays in many of his people. But that isn’t the right category for thinking about *satisfaction*. If I offer my sufferings in satisfaction for another, God doesn’t give less than what the value of that offering actually is.
>
> Now one might try to appeal to the condition of the one offered for. If I pray for someone and thereby “merit” a grace for them, the grace might be truly merited and yet inhibited by their condition. But this won’t work for those in Purgatory. Recall that Bellarmine and Martin Jugie—in accord with many theologians—taught that the soul is perfected in charity at the first moment of entry into Purgatory. And yet the Mass and Christ’s own intercession still only makes finite amounts of satisfaction—although the person in purgatory is perfect in charity.
>
> This is the heart of our soteriological difference. We assert that Christ’s satisfaction alone is our means of forgiveness of sin—full stop. And we argue that when he pleads it for us, no charge whatsoever can be brought against those for whom Christ makes intercession. So what remains is not the judicial sentence of punishment but the medicinal healing of God’s people.
