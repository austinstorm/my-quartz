> Another often-overlooked dimension of the contemporary traditional Anglican landscape is the limited success of Western Rite Orthodoxy as a destination for Anglicans seeking catholic continuity. During the twentieth century, a small but influential number of Anglicans concluded that the logical destination for those desiring apostolic succession, sacramental worship, and continuity with the undivided Church was Orthodoxy.
>
> Yet despite decades of effort, Western Rite Orthodoxy has remained numerically small and institutionally marginal. It has never developed into a mass movement among Anglicans, nor has it emerged as a major force within Orthodoxy itself.
>
> Critics of the Western Rite project argue that its central premise misunderstood the historic Anglican tradition. As Bishop Charles Grafton observed, Anglicanism already possessed “all that is essential to the Catholic Faith and Order.” Likewise, the Preface to the Book of Common Prayer presented the Anglican Church not as a newly created religious body but as a church seeking to preserve “the ancient Fathers” and the “godly and decent order of the ancient Fathers.” The historic Anglican claim was therefore not that it lacked catholicity, but that it had preserved it in a reformed and purified form.
>
> From this perspective, Western Rite Orthodoxy sought to acquire elements that classical Anglicanism had never entirely lost. The Book of Common Prayer already provided a mature liturgical tradition. The Ordinal maintained the historic episcopate. The Anglican divines defended the Church as “Catholic and Reformed.” Richard Hooker famously described Anglicanism as a church committed to antiquity, reason, and Scripture without surrendering itself either to Roman centralization or sectarian innovation.
>
> Consequently, advocates of Old High Church Anglicanism argue that many Anglicans who sought catholicity in Orthodoxy were searching elsewhere for treasures already present within their own inheritance.
>
> A second criticism concerns the practical reality of Orthodoxy in the modern West. Orthodox theology rightly confesses in the Nicene Creed belief in “One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church.” Yet critics argue that Orthodoxy’s institutional life in North America and Western Europe has often remained divided among overlapping ethnic jurisdictions. Greek, Russian, Serbian, Romanian, Antiochian, and other ecclesiastical structures frequently maintain distinct national identities and historical loyalties.
>
> While defenders view this as a legitimate preservation of Christian cultures, critics contend that it can foster the appearance of what Alexander Schmemann once described as the problem of “jurisdictional pluralism” in the diaspora.
>
> From an Anglican perspective, this issue is not merely administrative but missionary. Anglicanism historically understood itself as a vernacular and missionary expression of catholic Christianity. The Scriptures, liturgy, and pastoral offices were translated into the language of the people. The Prayer Book tradition sought to make the worship of the Church the common possession of the nation.
>
> Consequently, some traditional Anglicans have questioned whether conversion to Orthodoxy represents ecclesiastical continuity or a movement into a different historical and cultural tradition.
>
> The result is a conclusion that many Anglicans regard as uncomfortable but unavoidable: the crisis of Anglicanism has never principally been a crisis of worship or sacraments. The problem was not that Anglicanism lacked liturgy, apostolic succession, or catholic theology. Rather, the problem was the weakening of confidence in Anglicanism’s own claims.
>
> As Archbishop Geoffrey Fisher once remarked, “We have no doctrine of our own; we only possess the Catholic doctrine of the Catholic Church.” Whether one agrees with Fisher’s formulation or not, it reflects the historic Anglican conviction that Anglicanism was never intended to be a theological halfway house awaiting completion elsewhere.
>
> If Anglicanism already possesses the essentials of catholic order, sacramental worship, and apostolic ministry, then renewal will not come through ecclesiastical migration but through recovery. The task is not to become Orthodox, Roman Catholic, or something else entirely. Rather, it is to recover the historic Anglican via media and the Old High Church tradition that once formed generations of Anglicans throughout the Church of England, the Episcopal Church, and the wider Communion.
>
> The future of Anglicanism depends upon recovering confidence in its own inheritance. The answer to Anglican decline is not the search for a more authentic tradition elsewhere, but the rediscovery of the riches already contained within Anglicanism itself.
