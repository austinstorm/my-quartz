*These are notes taken for the Te Deum Music Fellows Program 2023.*

This collection of newsletters by Jim Jordan is also called "Rite Reasons: Studies in Worship" and were originally published between 1989-2007.

1. God's House is a House of Prayer

> The Lord's Day meeting of the Church is for prayer, first and foremost. It is not for evangelism. It is not an entertainment. It is not for miracle, mystery, or morality plays. It is not for an organ recital or a rock concert. It is not for intensive instruction. It is for prayer. 
>
> ...
>
> Worship is prayer focused on the person(s) of God.


2. The faithful worship of the true God does not come naturally to fallen man.

> It is pervasively assumed today that worship is easy, and that it should be easy. There should not be anything hard about it. It should be easy to learn and easy to do.
>
> Says who? Since man is estranged from God, it follows logically that the worship of God will be offensive to him. This by itself totally contradicts the idea of accommodating worship to the standards and feelings of unbelievers.


3. Worship is a command performance

> Since worship is a command performance, it is done at His command. We have been summoned by the King of kings.
>
>  I believe that the Reformed
> "regulative principle of worship" has often been misapplied, but the fundamental rule that we are to
> worship only in the way God commands is still valid.


4. Worship is family time

This is the most challenging for me to wrap my head around of the first four 'theses'. It makes sense sociologically but I'd need to try to read more around the historic understanding of 'outsiders' in church.

> It is important to think clearly about this. In Biblical religion, the Church is the first form of the kingdom,
> but in evangelical religion, the individual and the family are first. In Biblical Religion, the closed worship
> of God's New Family sets the key for all other worship: private devotions, family devotions, and civil
> religion. In modern evangelicalism, the worship of the Church is last, or at least comes after personal and
> family devotions. (Civil religion, of course, is a big, bad no-no).
>
> ...
>
> There is one other area we need to discuss under this point: Worship services should never be broadcast or
> telecast. Worship is private. If we performed worship as an hour-long (or longer) dialogue prayer with God,
> it would be obvious that broadcasting is inappropriate. Since what we call worship nowadays is generally a
> couple of songs, a "collection, " a short prayer, and a lecture, we don't feel so strange about broadcasting
> the entire service. Nevertheless, since worship is private, it should not be broadcast.


5. Worship should be beautiful

> This brings me to my second sub-point: We should first use the resources of Christian tradition in God's
> worship, not the latest fads of popular heathen culture. The Spirit has guided the Church for 2000 years.
> The tradition is not absolute, but it is our heritage all the same. To ignore the tradition of the Church is to
> spit in the face of the Spirit.
>
> The tradition of the Church is the believer's first culture, and his national culture is secondary. It is the
> living dialogue of ecclesiastical tradition with the continually changing contemporary culture that issues in
> genuine progress, and that can make worship both modem and beautiful. Modern evangelical worship,
> however, has cut off the tradition, and is left with voodoo and media-inspired gnosticism. 


SPICY:

> In reaction to this, there is an increasing move back toward tradition. Often this entails a blind and
> unthinking adoption of older ritualistic forms that are of dubious origin. Anglo-catholicism, for instance, is
> a romantic concoction of high church nineteenth-century Episcopalians. It has little real root in the tradition
> of the Church, isolating and majoring on some of the worst elements in the tradition. One of my own
> observations, and fears, is that the next generation is going to react against the cheap trash of present -day
> worship and adopt a lot of ritualistic claptrap instead. This may in some cases prove a cure that is worse
> than the disease! On the other hand, it may be a welcome relief. 


6. There are both private and public worship events

7. The heart of worship is Amen to God's three Gifts.

> The three gifts God gives us are (1.) the new person in the new community, (2.) the new word of
> redemption and dominion, and (3.) the new sacraments of life. 
>
> ...
>
> ...the progression of worship can be seen in the following
> ways:
> * Dispositional - presentation of Persons
> * Normative - presentation of Word
> * Situational - presentation of World (through sacrament)
> The new man hears the new word and makes a new world. 


8. Worship must be saturated with the Word.

An aside on fasting:

> Christian fasting is not an ascetic discipline designed to get the body under control. That is the pagan view
> of fasting. The Christian view of fasting is that we fast in anticipation of the feast to come. Fasting is
> eschatotogica1, not ascetic. We set aside some of the legitimate good things in life in order to be ready to
> greet the Bridegroom.


9. Love makes things beautiful.

> Each of these has a throne. The Pulpit is the throne of the Word, so we put a beautiful cloth on it. The Table
> is the throne of the Sacraments, so we adorn it with beautiful cloths, flowers, candles. After all, if we have a
> nice dinner at home, we use flowers and candles; naturally, we love to do the same when we have dinner
> with Jesus. And the Pastor's Chair is the throne of the Elder, so it is impressive and beautifully made.
> None of this contradicts the principle that we are to worship the way God says to do so in His Word.
>
> ...
>
> Second, the sad fact is that Adam seized at the principle of glory (Tree of Knowledge) without first
> acquiring holiness (Tree of Life). Thus, it is possible and all too often the case that churches majoring on
> glory and artistic beauty lack a concern for inner holiness and purity. In fact, though, holiness is the
> precondition for all true glory. Thus, if we have to give something up, let it be glory and not holiness.


10. Worship is praise.

> Biblical worship is vigorous. It involves training and continual effort. We have to throw ourselves into it.
> The Bible speaks of clapping hands and praising God with percussion instruments and other loud
> instruments (Psalm 150). In my opinion, the overhead projector is a happy development in this respect. It
> frees the hands and body for clapping and movement. The Reformers were "high tech" in introducing
> prayer books and psalm books to encourage congregational participation. We can also be high tech and
> make use of overhead projectors.
>
> ...
>
> The doxology, for instance, is misused as an offertory. The doxology is not addressed to God, but to one
> another. It calls on US to praise God; it is not directly praise to God. Thus, it belongs at the beginning, as a
> call to worship. 


11. Worship should be dramatic.

12. Worship should have an element of play.

> Having been encouraged to come, we enter and kneel to confess sin. The confession of sin in worship is not
> an admission of personal guilt so much as it is a confession of the fact of sinfulness. It is "confession" in the
> sense that the Apostles' Creed is a confession. Still, the fact is that we are sinners, and this is affirmed at the
> beginning of worship against all Pelagianism and against all self-delusion.
>
> Tension begins to break with the absolution, and breaks down more during the season of praise. Especially
> if we sing with vigor we shall find tension being removed This will issue into rest and quietness.


13. Worship comes in a context of death.

14. Worship should honor the Word by using a precise and accurate translation

15. There is nothing wrong with "Thee" and "Thou." 

> "Thee and thou" are not archaic English, as is clear from the fact that everybody knows what they mean. If
> you want archaic English, try Chaucer. "Thee and thou" were never common street English, even in the
> days of King James. They are not the equivalent of "du" in German. Rather, "thee and thou" are ~ forms,
> used in religious language and love poetry.
>
> ...
>
> Similarly, if
> preachers are not going to learn how to pronounce words like "saith" (pronounced "seth" just as "said" is
> pronounced "sed") and "ye" (pronounced "yih" except when stressed), then we might as well update these
> words as well.


16. The Psalter should be woven into the warp and woof of worship. 

This is the most compelling section so far.

> Metrical psalms are no substitute for chanted psa1ms, sung straight from the text. Text psalms preserve the
> poetic parallelism of the Scripture, and thus accentuate the dialogical and antiphonal theology of the
> psalter. Moreover, metrical psalms must of necessity be “dynamical1y equivalent," rephrasing ideas,
> omitting certain words, emphasizing others, substituting other names for God in order to make the rhyme
> come out, etc. Metrical psalms are like Biblical paraphrases - useful, but no substitute. Metrical psalms are
> one application of the psalter, but they are not a substitute for the psalter. 


17. Worship takes place on the Day of the Lord.

18. Worship is sacrificial.

> Sacrifice is something God does, not something we do. We commonly speak of "making a sacrifice" when
> we give something up for someone else. That "works-centered" notion of sacrifice does not do justice to the
> Biblical idea of sacrifice. The lamb led to the slaughter was not particularly thrilled at the idea, and neither
> was Jesus, who asked that if possible the cup might be taken from Him. When God comes to sacrifice us it
> is usually painful, and that is why singing the psalms is so important, because the psalms are full of pain.
>
> We would like to think that when the pain comes, we will joyfully accept it. Sometimes that is what
> happens, but think about it: If you are able to keep a cool head during your suffering, then you are not
> experiencing the fullness of suffering. The most potent kind of suffering, and of sacrifice, comes when you
> experience a "dark night of the soul, " when it feels as if God has deserted you, when the inward agony
> does not let up day after day, when you are weak and not strong, when you join Job on the ash heap of
> ignorance concerning what God is doing to you. This kind of sacrificial experience means that the Great
> Physician is doing "depth surgery" on you, operating at levels you cannot understand. The psalms are full
> of this kind of experience, and it is this kind of experience that Lord's Day worship is, in part, all about.
>
> In worship we open ourselves up to God's sacrificing work. We submit to the knife. Apart from our union
> with Christ, we would not dare do this, but in union with Him we can do so. We are privileged to suffer in
> union with Christ, although our suffering does not pay for the sins of the world. God's presence in worship
> is both a fearful and a joyful thing. 


19. Worship Is covenant renewal. 

> We
> can call the five elements: Call, Confession, Consecration, Communion, and Commission.
> 1. God calls us; we dare not come otherwise.
> 2. God glorifies us; focus on the Father whose Image we bear.
> 3. God instructs us; focus on the Son whose Word we hear.
> 4. God feeds us; focus on the Spirit whose Life we receive.
> 5. God commissions us; mission into the world. 


20. Worship Is dialogue

> The pastor, of course, speaks for God part of the time, though when the congregation sings psalms they are
> speaking to each other on behalf of God. The pastor also speaks for the congregation part of the time, in
> some of the prayers. The more prayers the congregation can actually pray together, however, the better.
>
> (In liturgical churches, the pastor faces the congregation when he speaks for God. This is called the
> sacramental position. When he speaks with the congregation to God, he turns and stands with the
> congregation. This is called the sacrificial position. Another way to do this is for the pastor to look up with
> hands raised when he speaks to God, and to look out at the congregation when he speaks for God.)


21. Worship is sermonic. 

22. Get rid of the divided service.

> Early in the history of the Church a deformation of piety arose that has usefully been called
> "mysteriological piety." Our relationship to God was increasingly seen in terms of a moral and mystical
> "ascent, " rather than as personal and covenantal.
>
> ...
>
> In terms of liturgy, this system was expressed in a divided service. The first part of the service was for
> everybody, but the second part was for Christians only; the catechumens were dismissed. The first part of
> the service focused on prayers and the sermon, which were deemed appropriate for seekers, while the
> second part focused on the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, which was only appropriate for possessors.
> I believe that this was a mistake. In the Bible there is no such waiting period. Those who wanted to come
> into the Church, for whatever reason, were admitted immediately. If they proved to be unsound and
> wayward, they were disciplined. The gates of the Kingdom should always be wide open.


23. A note on the Tabernacle and liturgy.

> Some charismatic liturgists have used the Tabernacle (or Temple) as a model for worship, holding that in
> worship we move from the courtyard to the Holy Place and then into the Most Holy. In the New Covenant,
> however, the rending of the veil means that the Holy Place and the Holy of Holies are no longer separated.
> Liturgically, we might have an entrance into the courtyard (the call to worship), and then after confession
> of sin, a second entrance into the Holy Place (the psalm of entrance). A third entrance, however, would be
> an error. 


### Conclusion

> People who have never sung chants think that they are dull and un-rhythmical. That is not the case, as you
> know if you have ever sung them in a lively worship service. First of all, chants are not to be moaned or
> mooed out, but are to be called out on pitch at roughly a speaking tempo. Second, when a congregation has
> learned a chant, and everyone is singing it lustily, it falls into rhythms based on the words.
>
> Once you know a chant, it feels good to sing it because you participate in the rhythm of it. The rhythm
> alternates duple and triple beats, according to the accents of the words. It flows along dynamically, moving
> inexorably toward the climax of the text being sung. 


> Q. 14: Don't you think, with the family under fire today, that we should emphasize the family in worship?
>
> No. Here's why. In worship, God tears us apart and puts us together again. That is what it means that
> worship is sacrificial. The best possible thing for your family is for it to be ripped apart sacrificially and
> then put back together again transformed. Outside of worship, God deals with natural families, but inside of
> worship, God deals with each of us individually as members of His family. Don't confuse the two.
