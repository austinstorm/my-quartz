Most people know of 'deja vu', which is French for 'seen before'. It's that feeling that we've seen or experienced something before, when we know we haven't.

We think of this as a material experience, a 'brain glitch'. (it is telling that most of our metaphors for the brain come from computing). But I would suggest it's also a mystical experience. Why is this disquieting, when a more 'materialist' explanation - that of something neurobiological going awry - is settling, almost comforting to us?

Each one is equally a bit of a hand-wave that reveals our priors. Either we believe the world to be material and therefore everything must ultimately have material explanations, or we believe the world to be supernatural and everything ultimately has metaphysical explanations.

We must resist this false binary. There's no such thing as a purely physical or spiritual experience, it's not 'either/or' it's 'both/and'.

We want to have explanations for things so that we can tidily file them. My favorite illustration of this is from the 1995 movie *Jumanji*. When the characters encounter the cursed board game in 1969 and the pieces start to move themselves, Sarah says that the self-moving pieces must be done with magnets. In 1995, Judy suggests that it must be done with microchips. While both answers are readily accepted by the other players, the later explanation of 'microchips' actually seems *less* plausible.

We ask ourselves what the 'explanation' or 'ultimate cause' of a phenomenon is, but we are unlikely to get one regardless of whether we pursue materialist or supernatural lines of inquiry. This search also seems reductionistic and a little insecure. If we instead make our inquiry along *phenomenological* lines we will be more likely to start to unlock the *meaning* of such strange experiences.

If we can accept that *deja vu* may be a mystical experience, than how much more for the opposite experience, 'jamais vu', which means 'never seen'. This describes when something that should be familiar suddenly seems foreign, like we've never seen it before.

*Jamais vu* can be quite frightening, and in some cases it is the beginning of a phenomenon called 'illusion des sosies', wherein people become convinced that their spouses or children have been replaced by doubles. But I digress.

While *deja vu* usually occurs spontaneously, it is difficult to consciously invoke. *Jamais vu*, by contrast, both happens spontaneously and can be rather easily invoked.

For example: if you repeat a word over and over again, suddenly the word will seem strange and unfamiliar. The word as a sign becomes 'unmoored' from the thing it signals.

Even more eerily, if you stare at your own face long enough in dim light, your own face can seem foreign - like it belongs to someone else. Some reincarnationists say that this is a technique which allows you to perceive your past lives.

I am interested in architecture and interior design. But I have found that one of the hazards of the amateur designer is that you lose the ability to 'see' familiar spaces after awhile. You become so accustomed to them that you can no longer perceive them the way people who are less familiar with them can.

For the designer, and indeed for the artist in general, *jamais vu* is a mystical experience much to be desired. However disorienting the experience may be, we need this experience, because we become overly familiar with the world.

I can no longer see St. Ignatius as I first saw it. Familiarity has led to blindness, and I am inured to its wildness. We cannot see what we have already seen. We need our eyes to be re-enchanted.

___

A post-script: A recent experience from a different context reminded me of this struggle to see things anew, and as they really are.

After Easter Sunday, one of my fellow parishioners remarked on the beauty of our Holy Week. They began attending our church relatively recently, and were struck by both the services and our communal life.

I spend a lot of my time thinking about how we might improve our services and communal life, which is both a part of my role and a function of my personality. But when he praised the beauty of the church, I was suddenly able to see and appreciate that beauty myself. I saw through *his* eyes, and the spell of my dis-enchantment was temporarily broken. We can do this for each other. *Jamais vu!*
