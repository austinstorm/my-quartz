**MANIFESTO ENTRY 1, 11/8/25**

We may feel like we are living in “interesting times” and can barely keep up with news of cultural and political upheaval. But simultaneously, paradoxically, almost everything has gotten more boring.

It’s been ten years since Kyle Chayka brought ‘AirSpace’ to our attention. This homogeneity of public spaces in the technological age, enabled by Pinterest and Airbnb, means you can now find homes and coffee shops that look the same in every major city on the globe. [LINK](https://www.theverge.com/2016/8/3/12325104/airbnb-aesthetic-global-minimalism-startup-gentrification) This is ‘AI before AI’, an algorithmic feedback loop that got tighter and tighter until modern design became dismally self-referential.

Since then, people have made similar observations about our cities (thanks to the prevalence of the zoning code-friendly, investment return-maximizing ‘five-over-one’ apartment building), our cars (which are now predominantly black, silver, grey, or white), our media (sequels and franchises dominate the market) - even people themselves. [LINK](https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/articles/the-age-of-average)

This also extends to the world of the arts & letters. Ross Douthat writes in *The Decadent Society*: “From the academic heights to popular bestsellers, from Christian theology to secular fashion, from political theory to pop music, a range of cultural forms and intellectual pursuits have been stuck for decades in a pattern of recurrence.”

Startlingly, this tendency to play it safe also seems to apply to human behavior. [LINK](https://www.experimental-history.com/p/the-decline-of-deviance#footnote-anchor-1-177315454) High-school students are half as likely to drink alcohol as they were in the 1990s. They’re less likely to smoke, have sex, or do drugs (including marijuana). People are less likely to live far away from the place where they grew up. [LINK](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2015/12/24/upshot/24up-family.html)

The danger here is not cultural collapse, but cultural stagnation. [LINK](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/are-we-living-in-a-time-of-cultural) As T.S. Eliot says in *The Hollow Men*, his poem about spiritual emptiness and disillusionment, “This is how the world ends / not with a bang, but a whimper.”

I also think of Father Romano Guardini. In *Letters from Lake Como* he laments how modern technique flattens the world into a single, efficient style, and in *The Spirit of the Liturgy* he reminds us that form educates desire. We do not know how to want beautiful things, because we have not been given the opportunity to inhabit them.

We are unmoored in time and space, alienated from any common culture that reflects the pursuit of the transcendent. We are neutered by the fear of the crowd and the dictates of the market. If you dig into a major vein of cultural heritage you may be accused of a ‘RETVRN’-style anachronistic LARPing, but if you borrow from other cultures in an attempt to escape bland techno-culture you may be accused of appropriation.

The answer is the radical contemporary. We must make things to reflect the glory of God, of the universe He created, and of humanity, the crown of God’s creation. When we do this, we needn’t worry about being transgressive to wake people from their apathy. In the same way we might reject ars gratia artis, we also reject “weird for weird’s sake”. God is weird, the universe is weird, and people are weird. When we shift from consuming ready-made culture to a ‘roll-your-own’ approach, things will necessarily ‘get weird’. There will be edges. It will be hard to market.

A chiastic conclusion: if you’re doing it right, it will be weird. But just because it’s weird doesn’t necessarily mean you’re doing it right.
