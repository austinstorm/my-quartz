### Alienation!

The modern world is flat and shallow and manufactured. Alienation is the order of the day, and it touches everything - music, religion, material culture. It is no wonder that folk culture has become so appealing. It promises connection, participation, *realness*.

The most visible expression of this interest in folk culture is on the Internet. Social media presents appealing images of folk music, liturgy, and handcraft. But we must be careful to differentiate between the genuine article and just another dead-end in modernity's house of mirrors.
### Clowns to the left of me, jokers to the right

Contrary to popular perception, folk practices do not emerge from timeless cultural idyll, nor do they function decoratively.

Instead, they were tools of cultural preservation in the face of pressure and social change. This bit is appealing to conservatives, who face a perennial temptation to LARP. But folk practices were also about communities without power organizing themselves both socially and politically. This is appealing to liberals, who are often stymied by in-fighting due to a lack of common culture.

Folk culture is about hope and community in the face of the difficulty of life or oppression and opposition. But folk culture is only folk culture if it reflects the practice of a real people, embedded in a place and time (a shared seasonality). Devoid of a real communal context, let alone a sense of place or season, interest in folk culture is reduced to the nostalgic and the symbolic. Thus deracinated it becomes either a tool to serve political ends, or spectacle curated to appease the algorithm.
### Sign World

The algorithm explains how culture gets narrowed, because it favors things which 'travel well' - presenting us with curated snapshots tailored to our interests. This is particularly insidious because it does this while simultaneously *appearing* to show us a broader range of things than we would otherwise be exposed to.

The algorithm explains the how, but the spectacle explains what such a narrowed culture might look like:

> In *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967), Guy Debord used the term to describe a society in which lived experience is gradually replaced by representations of it. Social life becomes something people watch rather than do and culture is reduced to images and symbols. He wrote “The spectacle is not a collection of images, but a social relation among people, mediated by images”

> In ordinary life relationships are direct. People work together, get in  arguments, celebrate, organise and share things. Culture grows out of those relationships. In the spectacle, that process completely changes. Instead of relationships producing culture, the culture begins to organise the relationships.

> Debord’s concern wasn’t necessarily that meaning or history would disappear altogether, but that they would become detached from lived experience. As he put it, “everything that was directly lived has moved away into a representation.” In this sense, folk culture risks becoming something we no longer make together, but something already there which people just watch and share. (The Folk Union)

In her 1977 essay collection *On Photography*, Susan Sontag wrote that “today everything exists to end in a photograph.” Now more than ever.
### Even Better Than the Real Thing

Is the point the appearance of folk culture, or the culture itself? The paradoxical pitfall is that the internet has trained us that the goal is accurate reproduction. But the more accurately we reproduce, the further away from folk our practice becomes.

That is, you can reproduce the sign of the culture while being detached from the communal life that gives the culture its force. The antidote to this is reproducing things badly. As soon as you do it badly, you're making it your own - 'appropriating' it, as it were - and not merely reproducing it. This is the difference between *performing* a folk song and *covering* a song.

Real folk culture is not easily replicated. This is why pubs in the US are not the same as pubs in the UK. This also alleviates some concerns we may have about 'cultural appropriation'. When cultural practices are adopted in a living way by a people that didn't originate them, they are necessarily changed.
### We have met the enemy and he is us

How can we tell if we have the genuine article or merely a cargo cult? One thing to look out for is nostalgia. Nostalgia relies on things being 'recognizable' and emotionally charged. Nostalgia itself isn't a problem, but when nostalgia is at the forefront it's a sign that the spectacle has taken hold.

In the conservative Christian worlds in which I travel, this looks like vicarious pride in 'robust congregational singing' or continuity of liturgical practice or whatever in a way that bypasses actually participating in the real. This makes those people much closer than they'd like to the members of the much-maligned Mall Megachurch, whose services often seem essentially vicarious- people sitting in the darkness, looking at beautiful and talented people up on a stage. The ultimate effect is the same whether you have a culture of excellence or a culture of vicarious glory through loyalty - alienation!

Folk cannot be vicarious, and there is no nominalism in folk. Folk cannot be exclusively individual. It has individual practitioners, of course, but it must have collective identity.

Sing, make stuff, participate in the liturgy - and find other people to do it with. The world depends on it, and no one can do it for you!
